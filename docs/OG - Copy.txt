Yes—“run it for longer and see whether it trends toward zero” is part of it. But one giant backtest can lie beautifully.

How the machine proves it does not work

Split time chronologically:

Development data → build/train
Validation data  → choose architecture/settings
Final holdout    → touch once, at the end

Then use walk-forward testing:

Train Jan–Jun → trade July
Train Feb–Jul → trade August
Train Mar–Aug → trade September
...

The agent only sees information available at that moment. No future normalization, universe selection, indicator fitting, or RL reward leakage.

Declare failure when:
Result	Meaning
Positive before fees, flat/negative after	No usable edge
One spectacular period creates all profit	Regime lottery
Small parameter change kills it	Overfitting
One-bar execution delay kills it	Unrealistic timing
Double slippage kills it	Fragile edge
Different random seeds produce wildly different results	Luck/model instability
Beats cash but not a dumb benchmark	Unnecessary machinery
Overall positive, most walk-forward windows negative	A few lucky bursts
Final untouched period fails	Research story rejected
Bootstrap confidence range includes zero	Edge remains unproven

The comparison should include at least:

cash;
buy-and-hold;
simple periodic investment;
one dumb trend strategy;
random entries with the same holding times and risk limits.

A complex hangar earning 8% with a 30% drawdown is not impressive when a dumb baseline earns 10% with less drama.

Stress it deliberately

Run the same system with:

fees ×2;
slippage ×2;
one-bar delayed orders;
occasional missing data;
partial fills;
several starting dates;
nearby parameter values;
bull, bear, sideways and high-volatility periods.

A real weak edge bends. A simulator hallucination shatters.

Important distinction

Your risk-veto fellow may genuinely work while the trading brain does not.

That produces something like:

Bad strategies
+ excellent risk control
= slow, dignified loss

Still valuable engineering—but not alpha.

The watcher should primarily:

detect bad/missing data;
detect behaviour outside the training distribution;
detect abnormal turnover, drawdown or execution;
freeze trading.

It should not immediately retrain itself after losses. Otherwise it learns from noise while simultaneously changing the exam.

About the Alpaca data

Precise data is a strong foundation. But data quality alone does not solve:

survivorship bias;
delisted symbols;
splits and dividends;
changing index membership;
bid/ask versus candle-price assumptions;
leakage caused by how features were calculated.

Then you can permit three levels of adaptation:

Level 1 — learning. Each creature updates estimates about which parts of its landscape are favorable. Your current pattern/calibration machinery already does a primitive version of this.

Level 2 — selection. Weak creatures gradually lose influence; consistently useful ones gain influence.

Level 3 — variation. New creatures are spawned with slightly altered dimensional weights, horizons, patience, risk preferences, memory size, etc.

Risk-constrained Kelly explicitly trades expected growth against probability of deep drawdown.
